## Bible prose

Rules:
- Plain sentences, present tense for how things stand, past tense for how they got there. One fact per sentence. Load-bearing facts also get a `::key:: value` line.
- Every rule entry has four parts in this order: the rule as it stands today, the rule it replaced, the reason it changed, and who still keeps the old one. A rule shown without its predecessor is incomplete.
- Competing accounts are attributed: "The porters' book holds that...", "The weigh-house ledger gives...". Where the world does not say, write `Unknown:` and what is left open.
- No mood adjectives. Say what it costs, who pays, who may refuse.
- No balance numbers in any spec; a measure in a page is a fact the world gives (a length of time, a number of coins) and is kept consistent through the index.

Example:

> The noon closure of the Salt Stair is enforced by the gate-clerks of the weigh-house. It replaced a closure at high water, which ended when the ferry company was wound up. The porters' book still gives the tide as the reason; the weigh-house ledger gives the licence fee. ::salt-stair-closure:: noon. Unknown: who first moved the closure from the tide to the clock.

## Fiction

Rules:
- Close third person, past tense, one character's knowledge only. The narrator never knows what the character does not (D-0006; the intake excludes an all-knowing narrator).
- The world enters through work: a load, a gate, a fee. A fact reaches the reader after the character has paid something for it (technique: decoder at the threshold).
- Sentences are mostly short to medium. The gravest sentence in a scene is as plain as, or plainer than, its neighbours. One concrete mood detail per scene, and it costs something.
- The ending settles the practical problem (the load is delivered, refused, or lost) and leaves the cost of the rule open. No ending where someone turns out to be chosen, special or secretly in charge.
- Length 3000 to 6000 words, in scenes of a few hundred words.

Example:

> At the weigh-house the clerk read the seal twice. Ewa kept her eyes on the scale, because the scale was the one thing in the room that did not have an opinion. "New pattern," he said. "Old crate." She waited for the rest. He stamped the slip and pushed it across, and the stamp was the wrong colour for the day.

## In-game text and barks

Rules:
- Proposed limits (the intake gave none, so these need your approval): a bark is one line of at most 12 words; a quest beat is at most three short sentences; a codex or sign entry is at most 40 words. These are text lengths, not balance values.
- One fact per line. A line names the rule as it stands now and never explains the old one; the old rule is for the player to find in the archive.
- Imperatives and plain nouns. No puns, no dialect spelling, no idiom that will not translate.
- A line may carry a price, a place, a time or a refusal. It may not carry lore the player has not yet met.

Example barks:

> "Noon. Not by the stair."
> "Seal's the wrong pattern."
> "Weigh-house closes at the bell. Not my bell."
> Sign: "Loads over the third tier: lifter's mark. Ask at the board."

## Dialogue

Rules:
- Nobody states the reason for a rule aloud. Characters answer the nearest practical question (price, hour, place) and the real answer is in the dodge (technique: the dodge carries the information).
- Officials speak in procedure: the form, the stamp, the next office. Porters speak in short clauses and numbers. Older people may give a partial folk reason that the archive later narrows.
- Danger is stated as a logistical fact, then the next step (technique: understatement under pressure). No exclamations in emergencies.
- Mild coarse language only where a porter would use it; nothing sexual (PEGI 16, no sexual content).
- No one explains to a newcomer what the newcomer can find out at a cost.

Example:

> "Is the south gate open?"
> "Bridge toll's the same as yesterday."
> "That isn't what I asked."
> "Same as yesterday, I said. Bring small coin."
