# Malazan description study: technique cards

Five technique cards from publisher sample material, written in the shape of `templates/world/authoring/references/CARD-TEMPLATE.md`. They are optional starting points for choosing a description technique, not a model of the series and not a universal voice. No source wording is quoted here; locators say where to look. Sources: [Penguin sample](https://cdn.penguin.co.uk/dam-assets/books/9781804995518/9781804995518-sample.pdf); [Tor/Macmillan book page](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250458544/memoriesofice/).

Coverage: the observations were recorded by an earlier session from these publisher samples only. They were not re-read when the quotations were removed, and nothing here reflects the full novels.

## Worn structures imply a past

- Effect: a place feels old and used before anyone explains its history.
- Mechanism: the description lingers on a weathered fixture and on buildings that were reused for a new purpose, so material change stands in for chronology.
- In this world: a customs house whose arched doors were bricked to half height when the river rose, the old keystone still visible above the new lintel.
- Avoid: naming the ancient event the repairs imply; a list of ruins.
- Evidence: Steven Erikson, *Gardens of the Moon*, ISBN 9781804995518, Penguin sample, prologue (Mock's Hold and Malaz City), printed pp. 3-4 (PDF pp. 32-33). Partly read (sample only).
- Used by: world-describer, location-designer, historian

## Damage gives a possession a biography

- Effect: an object tells you about its owner without a backstory paragraph.
- Mechanism: a carried instrument is described by its scorch and breaks rather than by its make; the narration does not need to name the owner to make the object matter.
- In this world: a ledger clerk's brass pen case, dented on one corner, the dent matching the edge of the harbor gate.
- Avoid: explaining the damage in the same sentence; making every object a clue.
- Evidence: *Gardens of the Moon*, Penguin sample, a young soldier carrying an instrument, printed p. 5 (PDF p. 34). Partly read (sample only).
- Used by: world-describer, character-designer, fiction-writer

## Compressed appearance sets a first impression

- Effect: the reader forms a quick judgment of a newcomer that the story can later correct.
- Mechanism: one dry, colour-plain detail stands for the whole figure as the observer first sees him, with no inventory of features.
- In this world: the Guild assessor who "arrived grey from collar to boot, even the ink on his fingers".
- Avoid: stacking a second and third descriptor that dilute the impression.
- Evidence: Steven Erikson, *Memories of Ice*, ISBN 9781250458544, Tor/Macmillan sample, Chapter 1, the first meeting after a servant announces his master is approaching. Partly read (sample only).
- Used by: world-describer, fiction-writer, character-designer

## Conflicting descriptors create unease

- Effect: a character reads as unsettling rather than merely strange.
- Mechanism: two descriptors that should not belong to one person sit side by side, and the narration lets the gap stand without reconciling it.
- In this world: a tide-reader with a nursery-rhyme laugh and a surveyor's flat stare.
- Avoid: explaining which descriptor is "the real one"; using the pairing for every antagonist.
- Evidence: *Memories of Ice*, Tor/Macmillan sample, the crater meeting immediately after the character is introduced. Partly read (sample only).
- Used by: character-designer, fiction-writer, narrative-editor

## Need directs how evidence is read

- Effect: a landscape feels examined by someone with a purpose, not surveyed by a camera.
- Mechanism: the point-of-view character investigates terrain with the body and with a practical question (is this safe, what happened here), so physical evidence is chosen by that question.
- In this world: a pilot checking a slipway by pressing a thumbnail into the weed-line to see how recently the water left it.
- Avoid: neutral panoramic description; clues the observer would not notice.
- Evidence: *Memories of Ice*, Tor/Macmillan sample, the barrow landscape as a waking character inspects it. Partly read (sample only).
- Used by: world-describer, environment-storyteller, fiction-writer

## Optional starting preset: situated historical description

**Status:** optional proposal only; not a global default and not user-approved by inclusion here.

Give each observer a current practical concern; let that concern select sensory details; use physical traces to imply a bounded past; distinguish what the observer sees from what they conclude; and explain only the context needed to follow the next action. Prefer causal, specific details over a catalog. Let need, danger or expertise affect how long the observer attends to evidence.

Original calibration examples (written for this brief; they reproduce no source names, motifs or events):

- "Mara tested the stair with her heel before trusting the handrail. New brass bolts held the cracked stone; the old holes below them were packed with soot."
- "He counted the jars by weight. Three had been refilled with river sand, though the labels still promised winter grain."

A project may choose different effects and must record its own acceptance, for example as approved exemplars in `authoring/references/exemplars/`.
