Ewa had the sack on her back and the delivery slip in her fist when she reached the Salt Stair, and the gate-clerk had his stick across it.

"Noon," he said.

"It's a quarter to."

"Then you'll be early for the next one." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the river, which was a habit of the gate-clerks, and which she took for rudeness until much later.

The slip promised delivery to the upper weigh-house by one. The long way round was by the tannery, forty minutes if the lane was dry, and the lane was not dry. She did the sum twice. If she was late, the fee dropped by a quarter, and she had already counted the whole of it.

In the tannery lane an old porter sat on an upturned crate with his boots off, drying his socks on the wall. He looked at her face and then at the sack.

"Stair?"

"Noon."

"Used to be the water." He turned a sock over. "Ask the weigh-house what it is now."

She reached the upper weigh-house at ten past one, wet to the knee. The clerk read the slip, then read her, then wrote something small in the margin. She asked about the stair on the third day, and the clerk found her the ledger. The ledger said the stair closed at noon for the licence fee. It did not say why the fee fell due at noon.

She copied the page number onto her wrist, because she had no paper, and went to find the next load.
